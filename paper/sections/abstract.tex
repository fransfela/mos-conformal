\begin{abstract}
No-reference mean opinion score (MOS) predictors return a point estimate with no signal of when to trust it, a risk for automated pipelines. We test whether distance from a reference feature distribution, measured for frozen DNSMOS P.835, NISQA, and UTMOS, can flag unreliable predictions and set per-utterance conformal interval widths. A leakage-controlled design separates feature-reference fitting, content-disjoint calibration, and evaluation across all seven NISQA Corpus subsets. Euclidean, Mahalanobis, and nearest-neighbour distances detect corpus shift (mean domain AUROC 0.615--0.690) but do not consistently flag high-error utterances within a domain (mean AUROC 0.433--0.499, near chance). Fixed split conformal intervals reach 90.1--91.8\% coverage at the 90\% nominal level. Mahalanobis-stratified intervals help some predictor--domain pairs and hurt others. A shift-detection score is therefore not, by itself, a reliable difficulty score for conformal interval adaptation, a finding that reproduces on an independent multilingual speech-enhancement corpus. Practitioners should treat feature-space alarms as domain-mismatch flags, not per-utterance error flags.
\end{abstract}
